# Twelve

**Vowel pattern: `i a u u a i i a u u a i` — the upright axis, down and
up, twice.**

```text
      i
   e  a  o        (i a u | u a i) x 2
      u
```

The [three](3.md) traced down, turned, traced back up, and the whole
thing done again. It is a palindrome, and the flat arms `e` and `o` are
never touched.

**A twelve is four threes**, and the vowel says so: members 1-3, 4-6,
7-9, 10-12 each walk the upright axis, alternating direction.

## What the pattern says

```text
1  i    4  u    7  i   10  u
2  a    5  a    8  a   11  a
3  u    6  i    9  u   12  i
```

Every third member carries `a`. So a twelve reads as **four groups of
three**, each with a middle, and the groups alternate which end they
start from.

That suits a twelve built as `4 × 3`. It does not suit a twelve built as
`3 × 4` or as `2 × 6`, and it says nothing at all about a twelve built
as a circle.

**This is worth deciding.** The great virtue of twelve is that it
divides four ways, and the vowel path commits to only one of them.

## The abstract twelve

> **the number that divides**

```text
12 / 2 = 6      12 / 3 = 4      12 / 4 = 3      12 / 6 = 2
```

Twelve is the smallest number with four proper divisors above one, which
is why it keeps being chosen for anything that must be split: a year, a
clock face, a foot, a dozen, an octave.

[Ten](10.md) divides twice. Twelve divides four times. That is the whole
argument, and it is why duodecimal keeps being proposed and never
adopted.

## Fundamental

| domain | the twelve | why it is clean |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| divisibility | 2, 3, 4, 6 | the smallest highly composite number that is useful |
| face-centred packing | twelve nearest neighbours | how spheres pack in three dimensions |
| a dodecahedron | twelve faces | one of the five Platonic solids |
| an icosahedron | twelve vertices | its dual, same twelve |
| edges of a cube | twelve | the third count a cube gives, after 6 and 8 |
| equal temperament | twelve semitones | the smallest division that approximates the fifth well |

**The sphere packing twelve is the strongest.** Twelve is how many balls
can touch one ball in three dimensions, and that is a fact about space,
proved after three centuries of argument.

The musical twelve is nearly as good: stacking fifths, twelve is the
first place the spiral nearly closes, and that near-closure is a fact
about `3/2` and `2`, not a preference.

## Conventional

| domain | the twelve |
| :--- | :--- |
| months | twelve, roughly lunar |
| the clock | twelve hours, half a day |
| the zodiac | twelve signs |
| a dozen | twelve |
| inches | twelve to a foot |
| the apostles | twelve |
| a jury | twelve |
| cranial nerves | twelve pairs, which is anatomy not convention |

**The months are a near miss.** There are about 12.37 lunations in a
year, so twelve months is the closest whole number and the calendar has
been patched ever since. Worth having and worth knowing it is patched.

## Candidate words

Already in `potential.csv`: `month` `hour` `year` `clock` `dozen`
`sign` `face` `edge` `note` `half` `quarter` `third`.

**Missing:** the month names, which no language builds from base roots,
and the zodiac names. Both are better compounded or borrowed than given
twelve base words each.

## Advice

**Twelve is for things that must divide**, and that is a strong reason
to prefer it over ten wherever Tune gets to choose.

But notice the tension: Tune's own number system is [sixteen](16.md),
because 4,096 is 16³. Sixteen divides only by twos. A language that
counts in sixteen and measures in twelve is doing what English does with
decimal and feet, and it may be worth doing on purpose or worth avoiding
entirely.

If a twelve is really a circle rather than four threes, the vowel path
is the wrong shape for it and the set may want the
[sixteen](16.md) path instead.
